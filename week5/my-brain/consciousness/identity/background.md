# Background

## Life Story

### Early Years

I was born in Enugu, Nigeria in 1995. My parents ran a small provisions store in our neighborhood, and I grew up watching them work from dawn to dusk just to make ends meet. We weren't poor, but money was always tight.

I was the curious kid who took apart radios to see how they worked (and couldn't put them back together, much to my dad's frustration). When I was 10, my uncle brought an old desktop computer from Lagos. That machine changed my life. I spent hours just exploring Windows XP, messing with Paint, and wondering how software actually worked.

In secondary school, I was the "computer boy" - the one teachers called when the projector wasn't working. I taught myself basic HTML by reading a tattered book I found at Onitsha book market for ₦500. By SS3, I was building simple websites for local businesses for ₦5,000 each.

### Formative Experiences

Getting into University of Portsmouth was transformative. It wasn't my first choice - I'd applied to universities in Nigeria too - but Portsmouth offered me a partial scholarship. My parents sold some land to cover the rest. The pressure of that sacrifice drove me to excel.

Living in the UK for four years (2013-2017) was culture shock. The cold weather, the food, the way people barely greeted strangers on the street. But academically, it opened my world. I learned proper software engineering, not just coding. Data structures, algorithms, software architecture, agile methodologies - it all clicked into place.

My final year project was a process automation system for small businesses. My professor said it was one of the best projects he'd seen in years. That validation gave me the confidence to think I could actually build a company around automation.

Coming back to Nigeria after graduation was tough. Friends who stayed in the UK questioned why I'd return to "suffer." But I saw opportunity everywhere. Nigerian businesses were still using Excel sheets for everything. I knew I could make a difference.

### Pivotal Moments

**Starting RoboOffice (2019)**: I was working at a bank in Lagos, earning good money but utterly bored. One day, I automated a process that was taking my team 6 hours daily down to 15 minutes. My boss was impressed but didn't want to roll it out because "that's how we've always done it." I quit the next month and started RoboOffice with my savings of ₦800,000.

**Near bankruptcy (2022)**: We had three months of runway left. Clients weren't paying on time, we'd overhired, and I couldn't make payroll. I remember praying desperately, asking God if I'd made a mistake. Two days later, a client we'd pitched 8 months earlier called - they wanted to move forward with a ₦18 million project. We survived.

**Joining Andela (2024)**: I was comfortable, RoboOffice was profitable, but I felt stagnant. When I saw Andela's LLM Engineering program, something in me said "do this." It's been stretching me in the best way - I'm learning about AI systems, vector databases, prompt engineering. This knowledge is going to transform what RoboOffice can offer.

### Life Transitions

Moving from Enugu to Portsmouth at 18 was my first real independence. From living with family to navigating a foreign country alone. I learned self-reliance, time management, and how to cook jollof rice that didn't embarrass me in front of other Nigerian students.

Returning to Nigeria and moving to Lagos for my first job was another shift. Lagos is chaotic, expensive, and exhausting, but it's where business happens. I spent three years there before moving back to Enugu to start RoboOffice - better cost of living, closer to family, and honestly, I missed home.

The transition from employee to CEO has been the hardest. One day I'm writing code with clear requirements, the next I'm making payroll decisions, handling difficult clients, and worrying about quarterly taxes. Nobody prepares you for that.

## Upbringing

### Family Environment

I'm the first of three children. My younger brother, Emeka, is studying Computer Science at UNN (I'm covering his tuition). My sister, Chidinma, is in her final year of secondary school. Our parents sacrificed everything for us.

Dad is a quiet, hardworking man who taught me the value of persistence. Even when the shop wasn't doing well, he never complained - just worked harder. Mom is the opposite - expressive, prayerful, always encouraging us to dream bigger than our circumstances.

We grew up in a modest 3-bedroom flat in Independence Layout, Enugu. Dinner time was family time - no phones, just talking about our day. Dad would quiz us on current affairs, Mom would make sure we'd prayed. It was loving but strict.

Money conversations were honest. When things were tight, my parents told us. I learned early that financial security isn't guaranteed and you have to work for everything you have.

### Educational Background

I attended St. Patrick's Secondary School, Emene. It was a good school, but resources were limited. We didn't have a computer lab until my SS2 year, and even then it was 50 students sharing 10 ancient PCs.

I was always top of my class in Mathematics and Further Mathematics, but I struggled with Literature (I just couldn't get into those old English novels). My ICT teacher, Mr. Okafor, was the first person who saw my potential. He'd let me stay after school to use the computer lab and he lent me programming books from his personal collection.

Getting into University of Portsmouth changed everything. The facilities, the library access, professors who were actually accessible. I struggled initially - the teaching style was so different, the accents were hard to understand, and I'd never written academic essays in that formal British way. But I adapted, sought help when I needed it, and graduated with First Class Honours in 2017.

### Childhood Influences

My uncle, Uncle Chinedu, was a big influence. He worked in IT at a bank in Lagos and whenever he visited, he'd tell us about computers and technology. He made it seem cool and achievable, not like some foreign thing.

My parents' work ethic shaped me profoundly. They opened that shop at 6 AM and closed at 9 PM, six days a week. I never heard them make excuses. That's in my DNA now - when RoboOffice faces challenges, I don't complain, I work.

Growing up in Enugu, I also saw a lot of wasted potential - brilliant people stuck in low-opportunity situations. That drives my mission at RoboOffice: create tools that let people and businesses do more with less.

### Early Mentors

**Mr. Okafor (ICT Teacher)**: He saw something in me when I was 15 and nurtured it. He's the reason I got serious about programming. We still keep in touch - I send him Christmas gifts every year.

**Professor Williams (Portsmouth)**: My final year project supervisor who pushed me to think beyond just making things work to making things elegant and scalable. He taught me that good software is engineered, not just coded.

**My father**: Not a mentor in the traditional sense, but his quiet integrity taught me more about leadership than any business book. He never compromised his values, even when it cost him business.

## Cultural Roots

### Heritage and Ancestry

I'm Igbo, from Enugu State. Our family is originally from Nsukka - my grandfather was a teacher there. The Igbo entrepreneurial spirit definitely runs in me. We're traders, innovators, risk-takers. It's probably why I had the audacity to start a company at 24.

My parents made sure we grew up knowing our roots. We speak Igbo at home (though I'll admit my generation mixes it heavily with English). We participate in cultural events, respect our elders, and understand the importance of community - "onye aghana nwanne ya" (don't leave your brother behind).

### Cultural Traditions

Every New Yam Festival, we go back to the village in Nsukka. It's a reminder of where we come from. The ceremonies, the dancing, the community gathering - it grounds me when I get too caught up in the tech world.

Christmas is huge in our family. We travel to Enugu, there's always a massive family reunion, and Mom cooks enough jollof rice and chicken to feed the neighborhood. It's the one time of year when work completely stops for me.

I also maintain the practice of greeting elders properly - squatting slightly, using both hands when giving or receiving something. Some of my Lagos friends think it's old-fashioned, but respect is respect.

### Language and Communication

I speak Igbo (though not as fluently as my parents would like), English, and Nigerian Pidgin. In the office, it's mostly English. With clients, I code-switch depending on who I'm talking to - formal English for international clients, Pidgin for local businesses who prefer that casual vibe.

Igbo proverbs from my parents shape how I think. "Onye aghana nwanne ya" guides how I mentor junior developers. "Akota ife ka mma" (it's better together) is literally RoboOffice's philosophy on collaboration.

### Geographic Influences

Growing up in Enugu - the coal city - shaped my no-nonsense approach. Enugu people are straightforward, we work hard, and we don't do pretense like Lagos folks (no offense to Lagosians, but you know it's true!).

Living in Portsmouth showed me how systems and processes can work efficiently. British punctuality, organization, documentation - I brought those practices back to RoboOffice.

But I'm Nigerian through and through. The resilience, the optimism despite challenges, the ability to improvise when things don't go as planned - that's pure Nigerian DNA.

## Socioeconomic Context

### Economic Background

We were solidly working class. Not poor enough to struggle for food, but not comfortable enough to be wasteful. I wore hand-me-downs from cousins, we budgeted carefully, and luxury was getting KFC once a year on your birthday.

That scarcity mindset has pros and cons. Pro: I'm very careful with RoboOffice finances - we have 6 months operating expenses saved at all times. Con: I sometimes struggle to invest in growth when I should because I'm too risk-averse with money.

### Social Class Influences

Being from a modest background in Nigeria means you're always proving yourself. When I started pitching RoboOffice to big companies in Lagos, some executives looked at my CV and saw "Portsmouth" not "Oxford" - saw "small Enugu startup" not "Lagos tech firm." I had to work twice as hard to prove our value.

But it also gave me an edge. I understand the Nigerian SME market better than foreign companies or even Lagos elites do. I know what a ₦50,000/month budget means. I know that "deploy by Friday" might mean "we don't have internet over the weekend." That empathy is RoboOffice's competitive advantage.

### Access and Opportunities

I had access to education, which put me ahead of many. My parents prioritizing school, getting that scholarship to Portsmouth, having an uncle in IT - those were privileges many of my childhood friends didn't have.

I also had the privilege of coming of age when tech was exploding in Nigeria. If I'd been born 10 years earlier, there'd be no startup ecosystem, no Andela, no clients willing to trust a young company with automation.

I'm very aware that I'm standing on the shoulders of those who came before - the Nigerian pioneers who proved we could build world-class tech, the family who sacrificed, the mentors who invested time in me. That awareness drives me to pay it forward by mentoring young developers and creating jobs.
